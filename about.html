As a lil teen, I used to binge-watch social impact ads from India and Pakistan. Something about the way they challenged norms and activated public conversation. And before going to bed, I typically had a chain of questions to interrogate mom & dad, until they were frustrated. That's probably where this started.

I grew up in a space where global and Western perspectives were more visible than local ones. Some realities felt distant even when they were near. Issues of representation, power and voice were that much more immediate having grown up with a minority background. For that, I became aware early on that meaning is never neutral, that it is deliberately molded, and that it can be reformed too.

That awareness and curiosity got me to study Communication Science at the University of Amsterdam. There, I explored how media, campaigns, news, entertainment, digital platforms, and the like are produced, received, and interpreted within social contexts. I also trained in empirical research methods including surveys, experiments, content analysis, and qualitative methods to study how (communication) environments influence perception and behaviour.

Since graduating, I have become increasingly interested in work that uses research to inform tangible change.I have been exploring how research and analysis can feed into interventions, campaigns, programmes, policies, services and experiences, particularly in the context of social impact. As I move forward, I hope to continue working on understanding and learning from how we experience the systems we take, whose and what perspectives are absent from decision making. And to build on research-based approaches that are more responsive, equitable and grounded in the realities of the people they affect.

Reversing the pinterest logic for an art-rental.

Factor IJ is a cultural hub and kunstuitleen in Amsterdam-IJburg. When I joined Factor IJ, communication across channels was inconsistent, and there was no coherent system for how artists or exhibitions were introduced to the public.

I led communication strategy and execution for the multiple organization pillars. I began by leading qualitative research into how participating artists experienced the platform’s role in their visibility and legitimacy, alongside an audit of existing communication channels to understand what was working, what was not, and where improvements were most needed. We saw that the audiences were engaging with artworks primarily through aesthetic references. And that to better preserve the emotional and cultural depth of the works, it was crucial to correct the object-led presentation to narrative-led framing, foregrounding process, motivation, and authorship.

I translated these insights into a revised communication approach across artist interviews, exhibition narratives, press outreach, cultural listings, social media, print and newsletters. My focus was on shaping how the exhibition would be interpreted before audiences physically encountered it.

This changed the way people interpreted and discussed the artworks, increasing the perceived value of artists in the rental system and generating a more cohesive narrative across platforms. These adjustments were reflected in platform metrics, with Instagram followers growing from 3,400 to 4,000, email open rates increasing from 40% to 62.7%, and we noticed more engaged discussions around artworks.

After tethered bottle caps became mandatory, they quietly disappeared from the streets. What used to be everywhere suddenly became rare and therefore noticeable.

For Factor IJ Presenteert: The Findlings, Maas van Duijnhoven transformed street-found waste into eight creatures tied to different Amsterdam neighborhoods, inviting people to look down again and reconsider what the city leaves behind.

For this exhibition too, we prioritised a narrative entry point over a simple exhibition announcement, framing street-found waste as part of Amsterdam's waste system. As a result, audiences were encouraged to engage with the work as something connected to “what the city leaves behind”, as opposed to simply “nice waste creatures.”

As Communication Lead, I developed and executed the campaign strategy and four-week work plan, coordinated the campaign budget, and managed the communications and outreach elements required to take the project from planning through to launch. The rollout included press outreach, cultural listings, printed posters and flyers, social media and a three-phase newsletter campaign. I also conceptualized the “Day Out with the Artist” reel, which reached 12.4K views (7K organic) on a 3,900‑follower account, about three times the usual audience.

Thesis: Testing VR as a tool for outgroup humanization

Research Question: To what extent does the level of immersion (360° video in VR versus 360° on desktop) influence out-group humanization, and to what extent is this effect mediated by narrative transportation while this indirect effect is moderated by in-group i dentification with one’s own social/ ethnic group?

VR is repeatedly branded as the “ultimate empathy machine,” a medium that can place someone inside another person’s reality to dissolve the distance between them. In my thesis, I wanted to test that assumption more critically, as in does immersion actually translate into greater openness toward people outside our own social group?

Using the documentary My Mother’s Wing, I conducted an experiment comparing VR immersion with desktop viewing. I measured participants' sense of presence, emotional engagement and attitudes toward a marginalized out-group after exposure to experiences of occupation and structural violence. VR significantly increased feelings of presence and emotional intensity, but participants’ attitudes remained largely unchanged. Immersion did not destabilize existing spectatorship, as people filtered the experience through their social and political identities.

The findings suggest that VR works less as a standalone empathy tool and more as a context-dependent medium. It can deepen emotional proximity but cannot, on its own, disrupt deeply held beliefs. This prompted me to ask whether participation, agency, and context could possibly be more effective in enabling engaged contact to foster overall change in humanization.

TrendWatching had a conversion problem. Free-trial users were skimming content without realizing the platform’s full value. The main reason was that the platform felt dense and overwhelming from the moment users landed on it, and many left before the value clicked.

To overcome this, I mapped the exact moment when users should understand how the platform supports their work and then built backward from that point. The result was Amplify Your EDGE, a guided onboarding experience with gamified milestones designed to make the value feel immediate versus delayed.

I led the design of Amplify Your EDGE, created all the mockups, managed the project budget, and the social (LinkedIn and Instagram) messaging strategy. The project was awarded Best in Class by a UvA lecturer and a former TrendWatching employee.

Greenwashing in organisations

Sustainability Communications

“Transparency beats perfection every time.” Most people are skeptical of corporate sustainability claims, but what actually makes a brand feel credible versus performative?

Through semi‑structured interviews with six working professionals, I explored how people evaluate sustainability messaging and identify greenwashing. Participants expected sustainability efforts but frequently questioned the motives behind them. Sustainability initiatives were often perceived as driven by profit, reputation management, or external pressure rather than genuine commitment. A common belief that sustainability and profitability are hard to reconcile, especially at scale, reinforced that skepticism.

At the same time, brands that acknowledged limitations and trade‑offs were trusted more than those presenting everything as solved. The project highlighted how transparency and honesty in sustainability communication can build trust even when the efforts are far from perfect.

What would it take to make a systemic injustice impossible to ignore?

We started by mapping the issue ecosystem, identifying key actors across international institutions, regional governments, and civil society, and analysing where narrative leverage existed and where pressure was being left on the table. This included mapping Pakistan’s relationship with the Taliban as an underactivated pressure point and outlining advocacy strategies using humanitarian, economic, and religious arguments to pressure stakeholders.

From there, the strategy centered on storytelling, using social media, short video content, and documentaries to humanize the crisis and mobilize public action. The idea included leveraging global petitions, partnerships with organizations like UNESCO, and collaborations with influencers and advocates to run cross-platform campaigns on Instagram, TikTok, and YouTube.

Even as a concept, the project showed how strategic communication and storytelling can make an injustice harder to look away from.

Media Framing & Visibility of Migration

Human Rights & Migration

Research Question: To what extent do articles about immigration in Europe from The Guardian differ in regard to the economic frame and the human interest frame between Jan 1, 2015, to Jan 1, 2020?

Our group project examined how The Guardian framed immigration in Europe between 2015 and 2020, comparing the prominence of economic and human-interest frames. The project was situated in a broader question of how news media construct immigration as a social and political issue through elements of second-level agenda setting. This was particularly important because, as Harsha Walia argues in Border and Rule, migration is often represented as a “crisis” without sufficient attention to the political and economic structures that produce displacement and shape who is permitted to move. Media framing therefore influences whether immigration is understood primarily through questions of economic costs and resources, or through individual experiences and humanitarian concerns.

The two frames were operationalized to develop a coding scheme. Economic framing included references to welfare benefits, fiscal costs and labour considerations, while human-interest framing included personal stories, emotional language and attention to how individuals were affected. Then, using Nexis Uni, we identified 880 relevant articles and systematically sampled 230 for coding. A chi-square analysis showed that economic framing was significantly more prominent than human-interest framing, leading us to reject our original hypothesis.


Individual Visibility Analysis.


Prior to the group analysis, each group member conducted an individual visibility analysis, narrowing the broader question of news media framing into a specific case. For this, I focused on examining how frequently undocumented immigration among Syrian refugees appeared in The New York Times, The Canadian Press, and The Guardian between 2015 and 2023.

The analysis showed that coverage was concentrated on the years immediately after the 2015 European migration crisis, with visibility varying greatly between outlets and declining in the later years of the sample. The results were constrained by the scope of the sample and database but underscored how fleeting sustained attention on a humanitarian issue can be in mainstream media discourse.
